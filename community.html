01 Oral History / Research / Advocacy

Oral History Project

Listening first.
Designing second.

After prototyping my device for deaf music accessibility, I interviewed members of the deaf community to understand their experiences with music and the barriers they face in public spaces.

Their perspectives revealed problems that extended beyond the abscence of technologies. Many barriers were systemic — connected to institutional practices, assumptions about accessibility, and gaps in existing policy.

I compiled these interviews into an oral-history research paper that examined those recurring issues and considered both technological interventions and policy changes that could create more inclusive musical environments.

Selected Interview Insights(Translated from ASL)

Three conversations that re-shaped my understanding.

01

"One time I tried to play music, like at school, and the teacher basically said, like, 'you're too slow.' And I'm like, 'Well, you know, I can't hear it. I can't hear the music that well.' So, you know, that was a challenge. So then, you know, I withdrew from the music class because it wasn't going to be a benefit for me. - Sarahanne Dambrosio"

Lack of inclusive educational policies force deaf students out of arts rather than providing adaptive accomodations.

02

“Yes, yeah, I have gone to a few concerts, but they didn't provide any interpreters, so you know, I just can sort of observe them... the vibration is just a feeling, right, but with the meaning, with interpretation, and with the visuals, we can understand what the music is about... - Anonymous”

Art venues lack inclusive and accesibility-based mandates, which discourages deaf community members from enjoying performances.

03

“So basically, the way that it works is, you know, the cup holder, and then it's almost just like a sort of arm that has a little caption device that you can sort of put in front of you, but the whole screen is there, so I have to keep scanning down and up and down and up... if everything could be accessible, I would be going all the time. - Rosanna Ascenzo”

Existing assistive technologies are poorly designed, making it difficult for users to have an intuitive experience.

Outcome

The project changed the way I think about engineering: accessibility cannot begin with a device. It begins with understanding the people, institutions, and policies surrounding the problem.

02 STEM Education

Teaching Robotics

Passing forward the opportunity.

Teaching younger students gave me the chance to return to the same kind of STEM community that first inspired me.

I volunteered at a local STEM institution, teaching young children robotics and helping manage a FIRST LEGO League team.

Beyond teaching technical skills, I had to learn how to organize a group, communicate ideas clearly, remain patient when things went wrong, and adapt quickly when students approached problems in unexpected ways.

Most importantly, I was able to share my own excitement for engineering with younger students and hopefully help create the same spark that others once created for me.

03 Musical Outreach

Performing for Senior Citizens

Seeing music come alive.

I had always understood that music mattered. This was one of the first times I saw its emotional power directly in front of me.

During my time in Boston University Tanglewood Institute's Young Artists Orchestra, I participated in outreach performances at senior residences and nursing homes.

I performed chamber music with my chamber partners as well as solo repertoire for residents.

Watching expressions change as we played — seeing people clap, laugh, cheer, and become completely engaged — made the experience feel different from any formal concert. Music was not simply being performed. It was reconnecting people with memories, emotion, and a sense of youth.

04 Community Service

New Circles

Clothing, dignity, belonging.

Something as ordinary as warm clothing can become an enormous barrier when someone is rebuilding their life in a new country.

At New Circles, I helped sort donated clothing for a free clothing program serving people in need, including newcomers establishing themselves in Canada.

The work made me think about the challenges my own parents experienced after immigrating here and how difficult seemingly simple parts of everyday life can become without an established support network.

Helping prepare clothing for other families reminded me that supporting Canada's diversity also means creating practical systems that allow people to arrive, adapt, and build stable lives with dignity.
